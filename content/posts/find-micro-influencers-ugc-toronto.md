Search "Toronto influencers" and you get agency roundups full of six-figure-follower names who won't answer a DM from a business with no ad budget. Search a niche hashtag and you get a wall of thumbnails with no way to tell who's actually local, who's active, and who's worth a message. Most of the difficulty in finding the right creator isn't a shortage of people — Toronto has thousands of nano and micro accounts — it's that the obvious searches surface the wrong end of the market. Here's where to actually look, and what to check before you reach out.

## Two different things you might be looking for

An **influencer creator** posts to their own audience — you're paying for their reach and their endorsement showing up in someone else's feed. A **UGC creator** shoots content for your channels and never posts it publicly themselves — you're paying for production, not distribution. A lot of brands conflate the two and end up disappointed: hiring an influencer for pure content production wastes their audience, and hiring a UGC creator expecting organic reach misunderstands what you bought. If you haven't sorted out which one fits your goal yet, [Micro vs nano creators: which fits a Toronto brand?](https://sixthdegree.app/blog/micro-vs-nano-creators/) covers the tradeoff. This post is about the search, for either type.

## Where to actually search

**Location tags, not just hashtags.** Instagram and TikTok both let you search a place, not just a word. Search "Toronto," then narrow to your neighbourhood — "Leslieville," "Roncesvalles," "The Junction." Posts tagged with a specific place are disproportionately made by people who actually live and shoot there, which is exactly the audience overlap you want for a local business.

**Hashtag combinations that filter for local.** A bare niche hashtag like #foodie or #ootd returns the entire English-speaking internet. Pair it with a city marker — #torontofoodie, #torontofashion, #the6ix, #tdot — and the pool shrinks to people actively identifying as local. Neighbourhood hashtags work the same way and filter harder: #queenwest, #leslieville, #junctiontoronto.

**Follow the tags on businesses like yours.** Find two or three Toronto businesses in your category — or nearby, non-competing ones — and check who they've tagged in their own posts, and who's tagging them. Someone already making content about a similar business, unprompted, has told you two things at once: they're active in your category, and they'll likely say yes to a paid version of what they're already doing for free.

**Ask the businesses you admire.** If a Toronto café or boutique has content you genuinely like, ask them who shot it. Most will tell you — it costs them nothing and it's a small compliment. This is the fastest way to find UGC creators specifically, since UGC work is less hashtag-visible than influencer posts; a lot of UGC creators don't have a large public following at all, because the whole point of their work is that it runs under someone else's name.

## What to check before you reach out

A profile that looks right can still be wrong. Thirty seconds of checking saves a wasted first message.

**Comments, not just likes.** Likes are easy to inflate or accumulate passively; comments from real people take actual effort to fake at scale. Open the last five posts and read the comments, not just the count. Do they sound like people who know the creator — specific, conversational, replied-to? Or are they generic ("nice pic!", emoji-only) from accounts with no profile photo?

**Local signal in the audience, not just the bio.** A "Toronto" location in the bio is easy to type and means little on its own. Look for evidence in the comments and tags: mentions of neighbourhoods, other local accounts in the replies, tagged locations across recent posts. An account with a Toronto bio and a comment section full of unrelated international handles is telling you its actual audience doesn't match its stated location.

**Consistency over time, not just the best nine posts.** Scroll back three or four months instead of judging the current grid. Posting that's dropped off, or a big follower jump that doesn't track with any matching engagement bump, are both worth a second look before you commit a budget.

**Past brand work, if any.** A creator who's done paid posts before will usually have one or two visible in their grid or highlights. Read how they handled the disclosure and whether the content still felt like them — that's a decent preview of what your post will look like.

## Writing an opener that gets a reply

Once you've got a shortlist, how you ask matters as much as who you ask. A message that just says "love your content, what are your rates?" makes the creator do the work of guessing your budget and scope, so you either get a quote padded to cover every possibility or a lowball that turns into scope creep later. Name the deliverable, the timeline, and roughly what you're expecting to pay — [Toronto creator rates: what nano and micro cost in 2026](https://sixthdegree.app/blog/toronto-creator-rates/) has current ranges by tier and deliverable if you're not sure what to name.

One more thing worth naming upfront, especially for UGC work: are you only using the content once, for the creator's own post — or do you also want to repost it, run it as an ad, or keep using it after the campaign ends? Usage rights are the single most common source of after-the-fact disagreement between brands and creators, and naming the answer is a two-line addition to your first message, not a negotiation.
