Grow a following past a couple thousand people and the DMs start. Most are real brands with a real product, genuinely interested in working with you. A growing share are not. The two look almost identical for the first three messages, which is exactly the problem: by the time a fake offer reveals itself, a creator has often already shipped free content, paid a "fee," or handed over more account access than they meant to.

This isn't a reason to treat every DM with suspicion. It's a reason to know the specific patterns that separate a real offer from a fake one, so you can screen a message in thirty seconds instead of finding out the hard way.

## The upfront-payment request

This is the single most common pattern, and it works because it inverts the normal direction of money. A brand offers free product, gets you excited, then says you need to cover "shipping," "customs," or a small "processing fee" before they can send it. A real brand pays to ship you their own product. It never asks you to pay them first. If a message ever asks you to send money before you receive anything, that's the whole conversation, and the answer is no.

## Payment that isn't really payment

Watch how the offer describes money. A real collaboration names a dollar figure, in your own currency, payable by e-transfer, direct deposit, or through a platform, against an invoice you send once the work is done. A scam offer tends to drift toward gift cards, cryptocurrency, or a payment app you've never used, sometimes framed as "easier for our accounting." None of those are how Canadian businesses actually pay contractors. ## Pressure to post before anything is confirmed

A real brand wants a brief agreed and, ideally, payment terms settled before you create anything. A scam account wants the opposite: post first, and the details, the amount, the timeline, the actual product, stay vague until after your content is already live and can't be taken back. If a brand can't tell you what you're being paid before you post, that's not an oversight. It's the plan.

## Requests for account access

This one is newer and more dangerous than a fake product mailer. A message asks for your login, a verification code that just landed in your email, or to install an app or browser extension to "track your performance" or "verify your eligibility." None of that is how brands measure a real campaign, and handing over any of it can cost you the account entirely, not just one bad collaboration. A legitimate brand asks for screenshots or a shareable analytics link at most, never a password and never a code.

## No verifiable person on the other end

Real brands are boring to verify, on purpose. Their outreach comes from a domain that matches a working website, the person signing the message has a name and usually a LinkedIn profile, and they're willing to answer a direct question about the company or hop on a quick call. A scam account tends to fail at least one of these: a generic email address, a brand name with no real site behind it or one that was registered weeks ago, or a message that goes quiet the moment you ask anything specific. Two minutes spent searching the brand name before you reply saves the rest of the process.

## "You've been selected"

Genuine outreach references something specific about your account: your niche, your city, a post they actually saw. A mass scam message reads like a form letter because it is one, sent to hundreds of accounts at once: "Congratulations, you've been selected as one of our brand ambassadors," addressed to "Creator" rather than your name, with no sign anyone looked at your profile first. If the message would make sense sent to literally anyone with a few thousand followers, assume it was.

## What a real offer looks like, for comparison

A legitimate brand collaboration has a named point of contact you can verify independently, a specific ask (one Reel, two Stories, a certain deadline), a price stated in writing before you create anything, and a payment method that pays you, never the other way around. None of that is a high bar. It's just specific, and specificity is the thing scam outreach avoids because it can't survive contact with a direct question. ## If you're not sure

Don't decide from inside the DM thread. Find the brand's actual website independently, not through a link in the message, and check whether the person, product, and campaign line up with what you were told. Ask for the brief and the price in writing before you shoot anything. Never send money to receive product, and never share a password or a one-time code with anyone, regardless of how official the request sounds. If a brand won't put a number and a deadline in writing, that alone is enough reason to pass, whatever else about the offer looks fine.

The underlying fix here isn't caution, it's structure. A payment method that holds funds until the work is confirmed protects a creator from a brand that goes quiet exactly as well as it protects a brand from a creator who never delivers.
