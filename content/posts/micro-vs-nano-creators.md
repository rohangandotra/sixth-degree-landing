A café in Leslieville with $1,000 to spend has two real options: one or two micro creators, or six to eight nano creators. Which one is right depends on what the money is supposed to do. Here's the actual tradeoff.

## The definitions

- **Nano creators:** roughly 1,000–10,000 followers. Regular people with an audience — a neighbourhood runner, a home cook, someone who reviews every patio east of the DVP. Most have never invoiced a brand before.
- **Micro creators:** roughly 10,000–100,000 followers. Part-time or full-time at this point. They have a rate, a media kit, and a content style you can preview before you book.

The lines are fuzzy and platforms draw them differently. What matters is the shape of the audience, not the exact count.

## What you get with nano

Small accounts talk to people they know. When a 3,000-follower account posts about your bakery, a meaningful share of the audience lives within walking distance of it, and the comments come from people the creator will see this week.

Engagement rates reflect that. Commonly cited industry benchmarks put nano accounts somewhere around 4–8% engagement, against 1–2% for much larger accounts. Treat those as rough ranges, not physics — they shift by platform, by format, and by year. The direction is consistent, though: the smaller the account, the larger the share of the audience that actually responds, because the audience is friends and neighbours rather than strangers who followed a viral clip two years ago.

The costs are lower too. Nano posts in Toronto commonly land between $50 and $250, and some nano creators will work for product plus a modest fee if the fit is genuine.

The catch: reach per post is small and content quality varies a lot, so you need volume for the math to work. Eight nano posts means eight briefs and eight separate payments — real coordination work if you're doing it over DMs and e-transfers.

## What you get with micro

Micro creators reach more people per post and the content is usually better — proper lighting, tighter editing, an actual hook in the first two seconds. If you need assets you can reuse, a micro creator is more likely to deliver something worth reusing. Expect $250 to $1,500+ per post in this tier, rising with follower count and production effort.

The catch: as accounts grow, the audience spreads out. A 60,000-follower Toronto food account has plenty of followers in Montreal and a surprising number overseas. That's fine if you ship province-wide. It's wasted reach if your business is a single storefront on Queen East.

## The deliverable changes the math

"Per post" hides a lot. A static feed photo, a reel, and a story set are different products at different prices, and they do different jobs.

A reel usually costs more than a static post — often double — because it takes longer to make and travels further when it works. Stories cost less than either, disappear in 24 hours, and are the natural home for a promo code because the link is one tap away.

So the same $1,000 could buy four nano reels, eight nano static posts, two micro static posts, or one strong micro reel with a story set attached. Four different campaigns, same budget. That's why "what's your rate?" is an incomplete question — ask for the rate per deliverable, then match the deliverable to the goal. Stories with a code when you want measurable orders. A reel when you want reach. A static post for most things in between.

## How a Toronto brand should decide

Book nano when:

- The goal is foot traffic to one location
- The budget is under about $1,000
- You want the post to read like a genuine recommendation, because it mostly is
- You can handle (or offload) coordinating several small collaborations

Book micro when:

- You're launching a product and need reach fast
- You need polished content to reuse in your own channels
- Your customer base is city-wide or online, not one postal code
- You'd rather manage two relationships than eight

Plenty of campaigns do both: one micro creator for the launch asset, a handful of nano creators for neighbourhood credibility in the following weeks.

## When neither tier fits

Sometimes the honest answer is: don't book creators yet.

If your Google listing has three reviews and no photos, fix that first — creator traffic will land on it and bounce. If you can't absorb a bump in demand (a kitchen already at capacity on weekends), a successful post just buys you angry comments. And if the total budget is under about $150, put it into seeding instead: give the product to five nano creators with no obligation attached. Some will post anyway, and you'll learn who's worth paying next quarter.

Creators amplify what's already working. They don't repair what isn't.
