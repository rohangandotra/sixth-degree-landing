How to Measure an Influencer Campaign Honestly

By Aaryan Juneja, co-founder of Sixth Degree · September 3, 2026

Ask a brand how their last influencer campaign performed and most answers are a shrug dressed up as an insight: "reach felt good," "the creator said engagement was strong," "we think we saw a bump." None of that is measurement. It's a story told after the fact to justify a spend that was never set up to be checked in the first place.

Real measurement isn't harder than that. It just has to be decided before the campaign starts, not reverse-engineered from screenshots afterward.

What "fake attribution" actually looks like

A few patterns show up constantly, and all of them feel like data while proving almost nothing:

  • Taking a creator's word for it. A creator reporting "great engagement" from their own dashboard is real information, but it's self-reported by the person being paid to make the campaign look good. Useful, not proof.
  • Eyeballing a sales bump. "Orders felt higher that week" ignores every other reason orders move: a sale, a weekend, a competitor's stockout. Without something that traces a specific sale back to a specific creator, this is a guess wearing a number.
  • Confusing reach with results. A post seen by 30,000 people is a real number. It says nothing about how many of those people did anything afterward. Reach measures exposure, not outcome, and treating it as the same thing is how campaigns quietly get renewed for the wrong reason.
  • A single in-app screenshot as the whole report. A platform's own "insights" tab tells you what happened inside that platform. It cannot tell you whether anyone bought anything, because the app has no visibility into your checkout.

None of this means the campaign didn't work. It means nobody can actually tell, which is a different and more expensive problem, because the next budget decision gets made on a guess instead of a number.

Three honest layers of measurement, not one

The fix isn't finding a single metric that answers everything. It's being precise about which layer of the funnel each number actually measures, and never letting one pretend to be another.

What the platform can track directly. A unique tracked link per creator, clicked before anyone reaches your site, gives you a real number: how many people actually followed through on the call to action. This is the one layer nobody has to take on faith, because it's logged by the link itself, not reported by anyone with a stake in the outcome. It proves interest. It doesn't, on its own, prove a sale.

What the creator reports. Reach, views, and engagement come from the creator's own account, because that's the only place they exist. Label this for what it is: creator-reported, useful for judging content performance and for comparing creators to each other, not proof of revenue. The mistake isn't using this number. It's letting it stand in for one it was never measuring.

What the brand can verify. A unique promo code per creator, or a simple "how did you hear about us" prompt at checkout, ties an actual order back to a specific post. This is the layer that answers the question a budget decision actually needs answered: did this spend make money back, and how much. Revenue divided by what you spent on that creator is your real return, not a feeling about how the campaign "felt."

Three numbers, three sources, three different jobs. A campaign report that quotes reach and calls it ROI has quietly swapped layers, and the reader has no way to catch it unless the source of each number is labeled.

Set it up before day one, not after

Measurement fails almost every time because it gets bolted on after the campaign is already live. By the time someone asks "did this work," the creator has already posted a generic link, no code was ever assigned, and the only honest answer is "we can't tell."

Two things fix that, and both take minutes, not tools:

  1. Give every creator their own tracked link or promo code before they post anything. Not a shared one. If two creators are pushing the same generic link, a sale on that link can't be credited to either of them specifically, and you're back to guessing.
  2. Decide what "worked" means before you see a single number. Is this campaign supposed to drive traffic, drive sales, or build a library of content to reuse in ads? Each goal reads a different number as success. Deciding after the fact means picking whichever number happened to look good, which isn't measurement, it's marketing the campaign to yourself.

A worked example

Say a Toronto café pays three creators for a post each: $150, $200, and $250, so $600 total. Each gets their own tracked link and a personal promo code in their caption. Over the following two weeks, the links log 140 combined clicks, and 9 orders come through with a code attached, totaling $310 in revenue.

That gives an honest ROAS of about 0.5×, revenue divided by spend, which by itself says this campaign didn't pay for itself in direct sales in this window. That's not a failure to hide. It's a real number a brand can act on: maybe the offer needs to be stronger, maybe two of the three creators drove all 9 orders and the third is worth dropping next time, maybe the content is strong enough to reuse as an ad even though the organic sales lagged. Every one of those decisions needs the real number first. A vague "it went well" gets none of them right.
